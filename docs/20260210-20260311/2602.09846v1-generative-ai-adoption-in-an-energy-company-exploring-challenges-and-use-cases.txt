Abstract 

Organisations are examining how generative AI can support their operational work and decision-making processes. This study investigates how employees in a energy company understand AI adoption and identify areas where AI and LLMs-based agentic workflows could assist daily activities. Data was collected in four weeks through sixteen semi-structured interviews across nine departments, supported by internal documents and researcher observations. The analysis iden-tified areas where employees positioned AI as useful, including reporting work, forecasting, data handling, maintenance-related tasks, and anomaly detection. Participants also described how GenAI and LLM-based tools could be introduced through incremental steps that align with existing workflows. The study provides an overview view of AI adoption in the energy sector and offers a structured basis for identifying entry points for practical implementation and comparative research across industries. 

Keywords: Generative AI, Artificial intelligence, AI for SE, Large Language Model, AI Adoption, Industry Case Study

# 1 Introduction 

Organizations across industries are increasingly seeking to use Generative AI and Large Language Models to support various business functions [1]. As of May 2024, a Gartner poll reported that 40 percent of respondents had deployed GenAI in more than three business units [2]. Recent studies also show that GenAI is moving from experimentation toward a strategic priority across organizations of different sizes [3]. AI has long been used in organizations for tasks such as automation, process optimization, and decision support [4]. The recent rise of GenAI and LLMs has increased interest in adopting AI more broadly across industries. GenAI can sup-port industry growth by offering autonomous systems that increase productivity, help new ideas emerge, and improve customer experience [5]. These technologies have the potential not only to enhance specific tasks but also to reshape organizational processes by enabling automation and agentic capabilities that support autonomous decision-making [6, 7]. Despite the growing adoption of GenAI, existing research often focuses on AI per-formance, technical capabilities, or individual-level productivity gains, while offering limited insight into how organizations practically adopt and integrate AI into everyday workflows [8]. In particular, there is limited empirical insight into how organizations identify AI use cases and how expectations about AI capabilities shape their inte-gration into work practices. This gap is especially important in knowledge-intensive environments, where workers engage in complex cognitive tasks that directly shape organizational operations. Examining how generative AI and LLM-based use cases are identified, interpreted, and embedded into organizational routines can reveal the social and organizational dynamics that govern adoption. Understanding AI and LLM-based use cases in these settings helps clarify how these tools influence work practices and supports the design of solutions that fit real workflows [9, 10]. This study explores the application of GenAI and LLM-based multi-agent systems in the energy sector to identify practical use cases and improve workflows. In collab-oration with an industry partner, the project aimed to support AI adoption in the Nordic europe region. The primary aim is to identify AI-driven solutions that address industry challenges and improve daily routine processes and identify new ideas where GenAI brings the role. Data was collected through semi-structured interviews across multiple departments about four weeks. We gathered insights into how GenAI-based solutions can be integrated into industry workflows. The findings offer guidance for AI adoption and process improvements in similar sectors. 

Contribution: The primary contributions of this research are: 

• The study identifies 41 AI-related use cases in a large energy organisation and links them to the operational needs expressed by employees. 

• The study prioritized the identified AI use cases based on their business importance, ease of implementation, and expected organizational value. 

• Two pilot cases were proposed to demonstrate the capability and practical applica-bility of generative AI in an energy organization. 2• The study provides empirical insights into the adoption of generative AI in energy organizations, offering practical guidance for aligning AI initiatives with existing organizational workflows. 

Structure of Paper: The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents related work on the use of GenAI in AI adoption organizations. Section 3 describes the study design. Section 4 presents the study results. Section 5 discusses the implications and 6 threats to validity. Finally, Section 7 provides conclusion and future directions. 

# 2 Related Work 

In this section, we review related studies on the adoption of GenAI in organisational contexts. Section 2.1 focuses on GenAI adoption at the individual and team level, where prior work primarily investigates task-level use, productivity, and collaboration. Section 2.2 reviews studies that examine GenAI adoption at the organisational level, including organisational determinants, practices, and challenges related to scaling and integration. 

2.1 GenAI Adoption at the Individual and Team Level 

Prior work on GenAI in the software field has primarily focused on individuals or teams using AI for specific tasks. Simaremare and Edison [11] conducted an empirical study to examine practitioners’ perceptions of GenAI adoption in the fast-paced soft-ware industry. Their study identified 22 practical use cases of GenAI adoption within software companies. Ulfsnes et al . [12] observed changes in collaboration by interview-ing 13 data scientists to investigate the adoption of GenAI for individual use. A study by Liang et al. [13] examined the utilization of AI programming helpers like GitHub Copilot by surveying 410 developers. The findings indicate that developers primarily use these tools to facilitate syntax recall, decrease manual typing, and expedite work completion. The study does, however, also point out several significant drawbacks, such as restricted user control and recommendations that fall short of both functional and non-functional requirements. Research on organizational determinants highlighted compatibility with existing workflows. Russo et al . [14] provide a comprehensive understanding of AI adoption dynamics in software engineering organizations by surveying 100 software engineers. The study finds that, in the early stages of AI integration, adoption is primarily influenced by how well AI tools fit within existing development workflows, rather than by traditional technology acceptance factors. On other hand, Polisetty et al . [7] examined organizational, technological, and environmental factors, and noted leadership support, readiness, perceived benefits, and external pressure. On other hand, Hossain et al . [15] studied textile and garment firms in Bangladesh and analyzed readiness through infrastructure, skills, and management support. Empirical studies further examine individual and team level adoption of GenAI tools in software development. Davila et al . [16] conducted an industry case study 3based on 72 practitioners and found that developers mainly use GenAI tools such as ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot to support coding and information search tasks. The study reports productivity gains but also highlights challenges related to limited context awareness and the need for human validation of outputs. Inshort, GenAI can act as a new kind of ”team member,” altering traditional communication channels. These findings extend Ulfsnes et al . [12] earlier point about individual adoption by illustrating a concrete team-level effect, and they complement et al . [14] insight that AI tools must fit into existing workflows. If GenAI becomes an informal first-responder to developers’ queries, teams might need to adapt their collaboration practices to ensure critical knowledge still flows among human colleagues. 

2.2 GenAI Adoption at the Organisational Level 

Recent studies examined adoption at the organizational level. Pereira et al . [17] stud-ied a Brazilian media company and reported productivity gains and concerns about reliability, security, and overreliance. Kemell et al . [1] conducted a multiple case study of seven European software organizations and identified 25 use cases and AI adoption challenges, including data privacy, legislation, and organizational resistance. Flyckt et al . [18] analyzed make-to-order manufacturing and identified AI tasks such as demand forecasting and predictive maintenance, and discussed data needs and explainability. An industry report by MIT Sloan Management Review and Boston Consulting Group [19] documented adoption gaps and the need for alignment, talent, and gover-nance. Hruby et al . [20] examined small and medium chemical enterprises and linked entrepreneurial orientation and culture to adoption decisions. Recent work discussed organizational practices and team dynamics. Solaimani et al . [21] identified leadership, culture, infrastructure, employee skills, and change man-agement as conditions for adoption. Sepanosian et al . [22] presented strategies for scaling GenAI in enterprises that focused on integration, trust, and business align-ment. Retkowsky et al . [23] studied early workplace use of ChatGPT and reported common applications and risks such as overreliance and reduced knowledge sharing. Kampik et al . [24] discussed Large Process Models in business process management with GenAI and LLM-based agents. Despite these contributions, organizational-level research on GenAI adoption remains limited, especially regarding how companies identify AI use cases and imple-ment solutions organizational functions. Existing work often emphasizes bottom-up adoption or individual perspectives, with limited empirical data from leadership or cross-departmental views [14]. Academic studies in software engineering note the need for industry collaboration but rarely address organizational governance or decision-making around AI [25, 26]. Our study examines organizational adoption of GenAI and agentic-AI through semi-structured interviews departments in one company. # 3 Study Design 

We used a qualitative approach with semi-structured group interviews to address the objectives of this multi organizational units case study. Figure 1 shows the workflow of the entire study. 

3.1 Research Questions and Goals 

The main goal of this study is to investigate the AI-based use cases and challenges in an industrial organization. We aim to understand for which tasks practitioners use GenAI tools, as well as their expectations, perceptions, and concerns when adopting these tools in their daily work. The following research questions were formulated to guide the study: 

• RQ1. What AI based use cases do organizations report, and what challenges do they face? 

This question investigates recurring barriers such as manual work, fragmented data, and compliance requirements that constrain the use of AI in daily operations. 

• RQ2: How can potential AI and LLM-based solutions address the identified challenges? 

5This question explores how approaches such as reporting automation, predictive maintenance, and RAG could mitigate these challenges and support organizational units workflows. 

3.2 Study and Instrument Design 

This research adopts a single embedded case study design focused on a mid-sized Nordic energy company with multiple organizational functions. The case was selected as part of our broader AI adoption research program, given the company’s active internal exploration of AI strategy and strong executive-level support for investigating organizational AI adoption. The company operates across multiple sectors, including energy trading, heating, solar, wind, and related services. It integrates more than 100 plus platforms and serves thousands of customers. Its digital infrastructure is distributed and relies on cloud technologies. The initial motivation for the study arose from management’s expressed need to better understand feasible AI applications and associated adoption challenges organizational functions. The study was conducted by a team of two researchers, who collaboratively designed a semi-structured interview guide, observation templates, and a document review framework to capture both technical and non-technical perspectives. These data collection instruments were tailored to the company’s context, with a focus on identifying use cases where generative AI and agentic solutions could address organiza-tional units’ pain points, improve productivity, and align with internal organizational targets. A four-week embedded on-site study was planned to engage with all relevant orga-nizational units, enabling sustained interaction with employees and observation of day-to-day practices. This design ensured representation across strategic, operational, and technical roles within the organization (Fig. 1). Interviews were primarily con-ducted in small group settings to capture shared understandings of work practices and cross-departmental perspectives on AI adoption. Participants were drawn from nine organizational units, summarized in Table 1. Participant roles and organizational units were reported. The selection strategy ensured coverage of core organizational functions, with senior and experienced staff and unit leads prioritized due to their overview of workflows, operational challenges, and potential AI adoption opportunities. A senior organizational representative served as the main point of contact. In most cases, the representative recommended and scheduled meetings with suitable participants based on their responsibilities and relevant experience. In some instances, the research team requested additional interviews to clarify observations or explore emerging topics. Interviews were primarily conducted on site according to the agreed schedule, with some participants joining online. At the start of each session, the study objectives, the role of the research team, and the overall approach were explained. This was followed by a brief discussion of the participants’ professional background and prior experience with Gen AI and LLMs. A multi-organization functions based designed approach was used to enable cross-case analysis and to ensure robust data for answering the research questions [27]. 6Table 1 : Organizational functions participating in the study

We conducted semi-structured group interviews facilitated primarily by the first and second authors. 3.3 Data Collection 

Data collection was carried out over a four period from March to April 2025. The research team was on-site four days per week, averaging six hours per day. The schedule followed a pattern where two consecutive weeks on-site, followed by a one-week break for analysis and compiling initial findings; then one week on-site, another one-week break, and a final one-week on-site period. The internal report was submitted end of April 2025 and the identified use cases along with the results were presented to the company on May 2025.

3.4 Data Analysis and Reporting 

Our analysis was informed by principles of thematic analysis [29] and comparative case study methods [30]. A total of 16 interviews were conducted across nine organizational units. Eight interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed, while the remaining were documented using meeting notes. Two complementary outcomes were produced from this material. First, at the organizational level, we identified 41 OF-specific use cases, which were consolidated into six categories: reporting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) based solutions, predictive maintenance, anomaly detection, budgeting and forecasting, and uncatego-rized department-specific cases. These use cases were reported back to the company as part of the practical outcome of the assessment. Three common priorities emerged across organizational functions: automation of reporting, predictive maintenance to minimize downtime, and enhanced forecasting for planning. 9Second, for the exploratory study and academic reporting, we conducted a detailed thematic analysis of the eight transcribed interviews. This process produced 166 quo-tations, which yielded 166 initial code occurrences. After consolidating overlaps, 125 unique codes were obtained. These codes were grouped into 14 categories as code groups and synthesized into five highest-level themes: (1) Manual and Repetitive Work , (2) Forecasting and Predictive Analytics , (3) Data Fragmentation and Integration , (4) Compliance and Validation , and (5) Organizational and Infrastructure Readiness . The most frequent code was “more manual work” (9 occurrences), while several codes occurred only once, representing unique but relevant issues.

4.2.1 Pilot Cases 

In this project, we developed two pilot cases to demonstrate the capabilities of GenAI and agentic-based systems and their practical applicability within the partner orga-nization. These pilot cases were designed to address real-world organizational needs and to explore how such systems could be integrated into existing workflows. The pilot cases are provided for demonstration purposes and were not part of a produc-tion deployment nor implemented for operational use within the company. Below, we present the two pilot cases along with their corresponding code implementations. 

Pilot Case 1: Email Clone System Email remains one of the most widely adopted communication channels in industrial and enterprise environments, particu-larly for customer support and service-related interactions. Most industries dependent heavily on email to communicate with customers, which requires employing a large number of staff to manually read, respond to, and manage incoming messages. This manual process is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly, especially for organizations handling high volumes of customer emails. Based on the challenges identified during interviews with the partner company, we developed an intelligent email response generation system as the first pilot case. The goal of this system is to support and partially automate customer email handling while maintaining human oversight. The proposed system functions as an intelligent 14 email clone, where autonomous agents generate draft email responses based on the company’s historical communication data and internal knowledge base. To implement this solution, we adopted a RAG approach. All relevant company datasets, including prior email conversations and domain-specific documentation, were embedded and stored in a vector database. The system utilized the LangChain and LangGraph agentic frameworks to enable agents to search, retrieve, and reason over the stored data. Upon receiving an incoming email, the agents retrieve relevant contextual information and generate an appropriate response that aligns with the company’s communication style and policies. A key design feature of the system is the integration of human-in-the-loop supervi-sion. Rather than sending responses automatically, the generated emails are presented to a human operator for verification. The human can review, edit, and update the generated content before final approval. This design ensures reliability, accountabil-ity, and compliance with organizational standards, while also exemplifying effective human–AI collaboration where humans retain full control over the final output. To evaluate the performance of the proposed system, we assessed the quality of the generated email responses using BERTScore, comparing them against reference responses from the company dataset. Initial experimental results indicate that the system achieves an accuracy score of 89%, demonstrating its effectiveness in generating contextually relevant and semantically accurate email responses. These results suggest that the proposed solution can significantly reduce manual workload while maintaining high response quality. 

Pilot Case 2: RAG-Based System for Autonomous Text and Data Retrieval 

During interviews with the partner company, a major operational challenge iden-tified was the management and retrieval of large volumes of internal files and datasets. These resources are primarily handled manually, making it difficult and time-consuming for employees to locate specific documents or obtain information related to particular problems. When employees require a specific file or need insights from past data, they must manually search across multiple repositories, which reduces efficiency and increases the likelihood of errors or overlooked information. To address this challenge, we developed a RAG-based chatbot system as the second pilot case. The proposed system enables autonomous text and data retrieval by storing all company-provided datasets in a secure environment using vector-based represen-tations. This allows the system to efficiently index and retrieve relevant information from large and unstructured data collections. Employees can interact with the system through natural language queries to search for specific files, documents, or problem-related information. Upon receiving a query, the system retrieves the most relevant content from the stored datasets and generates a concise, context-aware response. In addition to providing textual answers, the system can also return references or direct links to the relevant files, enabling faster and more accurate access to required resources. This pilot case demonstrates how a RAG-based conversational system can signif-icantly reduce manual search efforts, improve knowledge accessibility, and enhance operational efficiency within the organization. The results indicate that the system 15 effectively supports employees in retrieving both information and associated datasets, thereby addressing a critical pain point identified by the company. 

# 5 Discussion 

5.1 Summary of Findings 

This study examined organisational AI adoption needs in the energy sector through the identification of 41 use cases reported to the company, along with possible LLM-based solutions. The findings show how current work practices create clear entry points for AI. Participants described routine tasks, and they linked these conditions to con-crete areas where AI can assist. They viewed AI as support for reporting, forecasting, validation, and integration, and they expected small, practical improvements rather than large changes. The results indicate that employees can see where AI fits in their work. This provides a clear base for the practical implications that follow. 

5.2 Implications For Practice 

The results suggest that AI adoption should focus on cross-departmental priorities that align with existing practices. 

Reporting. Manual reporting was identified as one of the most time-consuming tasks. Participants directly mentioned expectations that reporting could be automated through AI. Approaches based on retrieval-augmented generation can help unify access to dispersed documentation and reduce manual duplication 1. Such capabilities could allow reporting work to shift from data preparation to analysis and interpretation. 

Forecasting and Predictive Maintenance. Forecasting and maintenance emerged as areas where participants expected models to provide more accurate and proactive insights. Participants described the need for more stable and transparent forecasting practices to support multi-horizon planning and coordination organiza-tional functions. Predictive maintenance was similarly emphasized, with participants noting that reactive maintenance creates operational disruptions. Deploying predic-tive maintenance capabilities across units such as Energy Trading and Electricity Distribution could support more stable operations and cost control. 

Data Integration. Fragmentation across different data sources was identified as a barrier. Participants mentioned the need for integration between systems. This reflects findings from prior enterprise adoption research, where interoperability and system silos were identified as major constraints on scaling AI initiatives [1]. Standardized data access mechanisms may help organizations access distributed data in a uniform way. 

Compliance and Validation. Validation tasks and audit preparation were high-lighted as repetitive burdens. Participants anticipated that AI could reduce compliance workload through anomaly detection and risk assessment support. Automating parts of compliance validation could allow staff to focus on interpretation and decision-making rather than manual verification.

16 Organizational and Infrastructure Readiness. Participant responses showed interest in using existing systems and resources as a foundation for adopting AI-based tools. Organizations can build readiness by developing data practices, strengthening internal coordination, and aligning AI use with established processes. Introducing automation incrementally through modular solutions that integrate with current work-flows may provide a practical adoption path without requiring extensive structural changes. Overall, these implications highlight that while technical capabilities exist, adoption depends on organizational readiness, cross-departmental coordination, and the ability to prioritize shared needs such as reporting, forecasting, and predictive maintenance. 

5.3 Implications for Research 

This study is conducted on the energy sector. The five identified themes, manual work, forecasting, data integration, compliance, and infrastructure readiness—translate into system-level needs that span departments. This suggests that AI adoption challenges in non-software organisations are also requirements-intensive, making them relevant for software engineering research. The findings also reinforce observations from earlier studies [1], which showed that adoption depends on organisational readiness, user trust, and infrastructure integra-tion. We identifiy the usecases and their possible solutions where, and how these can be mapped into technical use cases and organisational priorities. Future research should build on three directions. First, more detailed studies are needed to see how organisations turn identified use cases into actual system requirements and deployed solutions. Second, cross-sector comparisons can show which adoption barriers are unique to energy utilities and which are shared with manu-facturing or services. Third, replication with additional cases would allow stronger generalisation and cumulative evidence on AI adoption across complex organisations. 

# 6 Threats to Validity 

Our study is subject to some threats to validity, which are discussed here to provide a clear understanding of the limitations. 

Construct Validity. Interview protocols and observation templates were designed to capture AI-related use cases and challenges. Eight interviews were audio-recorded with consent, while for the remaining interviews detailed notes were taken. This dif-ference in data quality may have influenced the richness of the coding. To mitigate bias, data sources also included internal artefacts and direct observations, which were used to cross-check interview findings. 

Internal Validity. Interviews were the primary data source, supported by researcher notes and selected internal artefacts for context. The first and second authors conducted the data collection and led the coding work. To reduce inter-pretive bias, the authors held discussion sessions, compared coding decisions, and iteratively refined the codebook and themes. These procedures were used to increase the consistency and transparency of the analysis. 17 External Validity. The findings are based on a single industrial company, though data were collected across nine organizational units. Consequently, the results may not be generalizable to all organizations or sectors. Nevertheless, the identification of recurring themes—such as reporting automation, predictive maintenance, and forecasting—indicates that these findings may be transferable to similar multi-unit organizations in data-intensive industries. 

Reliability. Most interviews were recorded and transcribed. In a few cases, where recording was not possible due to organisational constraints, detailed researcher notes were used. These notes followed a consistent structure and were reviewed immediately after each session. This variation introduces differences in the level of textual detail, which may influence reproducibility. To support reliability, the full coding structure, including codes, categories, and themes, can be provided as supplementary material. 

Ethical Considerations. All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. The study followed company NDA requirements. Sensitive oper-ational details and quotations that could reveal confidential business information were excluded or anonymised to ensure participant and organisational confidentiality. In addition to participant anonymization, organizational context was reported at a generalized level to prevent unintended identification. 

# 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

This study examined how employees in an energy organisation view the use of AI in their operational work. The interview analysis showed consistent interest in using AI to support reporting activities, forecasting tasks, data handling, and maintenance-related work. Participants described how GenAI and LLM-based tools could be introduced through incremental steps that build on existing workflows. This study contributes a multi-department, empirically grounded perspective on organisational GenAI adoption, showing how employees translate routine operational challenges into expectations for AI support. By connecting these articulated needs to concrete use cases and cross-departmental patterns, the study advances understanding of how AI adoption emerges within existing workflows rather than through top-down technological transformation. Future work can examine how these findings generalize to other organisational contexts, how AI-supported practices evolve during deployment, and how agentic sys-tems are integrated into daily operations. Longitudinal studies and multi-company investigations would be useful for understanding how adoption progresses over time.
